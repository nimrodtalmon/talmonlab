Steering a Living Allocator
with Scarce Attention

Which mechanism should a community use
to keep an AI-run fund on course?

Nimrod Talmon · Ben-Gurion University · draft, November 18, 2026

Motivation

An AI splits a stream of funding

Money flows all the time; when the split goes wrong, someone should notice.

Motivation

A real case: Deep Funding, round GG24

An AI splits $350k among 98 open-source repos; human jurors compare pairs.

scarce

About 50 jurors gave about 500 judgments; motivation was the main constraint.

noisy

Jurors disagreed on which project ranks higher 40% of the time.

fragile

One answer rating a project 999× another moved about a quarter of the funding.

wanted

The trial’s analysis asks for continuous funding with occasional juror input.

Model

The fund, the AI, the members

Projects \(1, \dots, m\); funding flows continuously. The split \(\mathrm{split}_t\) gives each project’s fraction at time \(t\).

An AI sets the split: right on most projects, wrong on some for a long time.

Members \(1, \dots, n\); view \(\mathrm{view}_i\): the split \(i\) wants on reflection. Some maintain a project.

Members notice problems now and then and may flag a project as too low or too high.

The AI’s split over time; one project is over-funded.

Model

Questions and answers

A question shows a member two projects \(a, b\) and their current amounts.

The answer follows the member’s view, with noise: more to \(a\), about right, or more to \(b\).

Exaggeration: in the moment, members may want log-ratios inflated by \(\mathrm{bel} \ge 1\); typing a number may inflate them further, by \(\mathrm{infl}\).

Attention: answered questions per day. Flags are nearly free.

Model

What an answer does

Nudge: “more to \(a\)” multiplies \(\tfrac{\mathrm{split}(a)}{\mathrm{split}(b)}\) by a fixed factor \(e^{\mathrm{step}}\); “more to \(b\)” divides by it.

Multiplier (the round’s format): the member types how many times more \(a\) deserves; the ratio moves part of the way there.

A nudge uses only an answer’s direction; a multiplier uses its size.

Simulated answers on one pair.

What we want

Accuracy, little attention, robustness

Target \(\mathrm{target}\): every pair gets the ratio the median member wants on reflection.

Accuracy: little misallocated funding,

\(\int_0^T \tfrac12 \textstyle\sum_j |\mathrm{split}_t(j) - \mathrm{target}(j)|\, dt\)

Little attention: few answers per day.

Robustness: bounded influence per answer; typing-proof; capture-proof.

Accuracy and attention conflict: we want mechanisms on the frontier.

Theorem 1

Nudges are bounded, typing-proof, agenda-free

Under nudges, with small steps:

(a) one answer moves a ratio by a factor of at most \(e^{\mathrm{step}}\);

(b) each pair rests at its median wanted ratio, inflated by \(\mathrm{bel}\), not by \(\mathrm{infl}\);

(c) if one split fits all medians, then it is the rest point, whatever is asked.

Multipliers fail (a) and (b): they rest at \({\mathrm{bel} \times \mathrm{infl}}\) times the mean.

Nine wanted ratios; \(\mathrm{bel} = 1\), \(\mathrm{infl} = 2.7\)

Theorem 2

Open forums are captured; recusal fixes it

Suppose a fraction \(\mathrm{frac}_j\) of the answers about project \(j\) come from its maintainers, who want \(j\) higher by a factor \(e^{\mathrm{bias}}\).

Then \(j\)’s resting share grows with \(\mathrm{frac}_j\), up to that factor above the consensus.

Recusal sets \(\mathrm{frac}_j = 0\); in an open forum, \(\mathrm{frac}_j \approx 1\) for projects nobody else looks at.

\(\mathrm{bias} = 1\); wanted log-ratios normal, standard deviation 0.71.

Theorem 3

Accuracy for attention

One pair, questions at rate \(q\); the median drifts with volatility \(\mathrm{vol}\); answer noise has density \(\mathrm{dens}\) at zero. Nudges’ mean-square error is about

\(\displaystyle\frac{q \cdot \mathrm{step}^2 + \mathrm{vol}^2}{4 \cdot q \cdot \mathrm{step} \cdot \mathrm{dens}}\)

Best step: \(\mathrm{vol}/\sqrt{q}\), smaller with more attention.

Multipliers do at most \(\sqrt{\pi/2} \approx 1.25\) times better, only for honest, normal answers.

Dots: simulation; curves: formula; 1 to 64 answers a day.

Comparison

Flag and sample is the most accurate

At every attention budget, flag and sample misallocates the least, slightly ahead of share-weighted: 9.7% at 5 answers a day, 8.1% at 10; the AI alone, 19.8%.

Simulation: the 98 repos of the round; AI off by about 4.5× on 10% of them; 150 members, 50 maintainers; 12 runs of 180 days. Dashed: share-weighted.

Comparison

Humans help unless beliefs exaggerate a lot

Typed numbers are inflated by 2.7. If beliefs carry at most 2 of it, then flag and sample still beats the AI (15.9% against 19.8%); if all of it, nothing does.

Simulation, same world, best steps. With strong belief exaggeration, more attention hurts.

Conclusion

Our advice

1

Flags pick the questions; random non-maintainers answer

2

Answers give a direction; each moves a ratio by 5–10%

3

About 5 answers a day halve the AI’s error

4

Avoid open forums and typed multipliers

5

Caveat: first check that beliefs are not too exaggerated
